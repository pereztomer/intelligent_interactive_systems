# Theory Analysis for Presentation Rehearsal Coach

## Overview
This document analyzes relevant theories from the Intelligent Interactive Systems course lectures and their application to the Presentation Rehearsal Coach system.

---

## Lecture 01: IIS Intro (2025)

### Key Theories Identified

#### 1. **Barbara Grosz (2012) - Central Question**
**Citation**: Grosz, B. J. (2012). "What Question Would Turing Pose Today?"

**Core Question**: "How can humans and AI systems think well together?"

**Relevance to Our System**: HIGH
- Our system directly addresses this question by creating a collaborative relationship between human presenters and AI coaching
- The hybrid architecture (algorithmic precision + AI interpretation) exemplifies human-AI collaboration
- The system enhances human capabilities rather than replacing them

**Application**:
- User practices presentation (human expertise)
- AI provides expert-level feedback (AI expertise)
- Together they achieve better outcomes than either alone

---

#### 2. **Amershi et al. (2019) - Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction**
**Citation**: Amershi, S., et al. (2019). Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction. CHI 2019.

**Additional Sources Mentioned**:
- Weisz, J. D., et al. (2024) - Updated guidelines
- Zhao, J., et al. (2025) - Recent guidelines
- Horvitz, E. (1999) - Historical reference
- Shneiderman, B. (2020) - Design principles

**Relevance to Our System**: VERY HIGH

**10 Core Guidelines (Adapted from Amershi et al., 2019; Weisz et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025)**:

1. **Clarify Capabilities** ✅
   - System clearly states it provides presentation feedback on pacing, filler words, and clarity
   - Users understand what the system can and cannot do

2. **Build Mental Models** ✅
   - Users see both raw metrics and AI interpretation
   - System shows what it measures (audio + navigation + content)
   - Users understand the feedback generation process

3. **Handle Uncertainty** ✅
   - System shows metrics even if AI fails (graceful degradation)
   - Uncertainty in AI interpretation is acknowledged

4. **Balance Autonomy & Control** ✅
   - User controls when to record and analyze
   - System doesn't interrupt during presentation
   - User decides when to view feedback

5. **Close the Feedback Loop** ✅
   - Immediate feedback after each attempt
   - Users can see the impact of their actions
   - Multiple attempts allow iterative improvement

6. **Explain Decisions** ✅
   - AI explains why issues matter (e.g., "high filler words + fast pace = nervousness")
   - Feedback is contextual and educational, not just metric reporting

7. **Fail Gracefully** ✅
   - If AI fails, raw metrics are still available
   - System doesn't break if one component fails
   - Error handling is built into the architecture

8. **Calibrate Trust** ✅
   - Shows both objective metrics and AI interpretation
   - Users can verify AI reasoning
   - Transparency builds appropriate trust levels

9. **Embed Ethics & Fairness** ✅
   - Focuses on objective, measurable metrics
   - No bias in what is measured
   - Fair evaluation based on presentation skills, not personal characteristics

10. **Support Reflection** ✅
    - Session-level analysis tracks trends
    - Users can reflect on progress over time
    - Identifies patterns and improvement areas

---

#### 3. **Nass & Reeves (1996) - Social Rules Applied to Computers**
**Citation**: Nass, C., & Reeves, B. (1996). The Media Equation: How People Treat Computers, Television, and New Media Like Real People and Places.

**Key Finding**: People apply social rules and expectations to computers

**Relevance to Our System**: HIGH

**Application**:
- **Avoiding Clippy's Failure**: Our system avoids over-socialization
  - Doesn't interrupt during presentation (unlike Clippy)
  - Provides feedback only when requested
  - Acts as a tool/assistant, not an intrusive agent
  
- **Learning from Google Smart Reply**: 
  - Provides value without being annoying
  - Contextually appropriate (feedback after attempt, not during)
  - Socially invisible when not needed

**Design Implications**:
- System respects user's presentation flow
- Feedback is provided at appropriate times
- No unnecessary social cues or interruptions

---

#### 4. **Human-AI Collaboration Framework**
**Three Levels of Collaboration**:

1. **Tool**: AI performs tasks automatically
2. **Assistant**: AI enhances productivity and supports tasks ✅ **Our System**
3. **True Collaborator**: AI works alongside humans, leveraging mutual strengths

**Relevance to Our System**: HIGH

**Our System as Assistant**:
- Enhances user's presentation practice
- Provides expert-level feedback that would be difficult to obtain otherwise
- Supports user's goals without replacing human expertise
- Hybrid architecture leverages complementary strengths

## Lecture 03: Human-Robot Teamwork (2025)

### Key Theories Identified

#### 1. **Shared Mental Models (Marks et al., 2002)**
**Citation**: Marks, M. A., Sabella, M. J., Burke, C. S., & Zaccaro, S. J. (2002). The impact of cross-training on team effectiveness. Journal of Applied Psychology, 87(1), 3.

**Definition**: "The content and organization of inter-role knowledge held by team members within a performance setting."
- Procedural knowledge about how team members should work together
- Information about who should do what at particular points in time

**Relevance to Our System**: MEDIUM-HIGH

**Application**:
- **User Mental Model**: Users develop understanding of:
  - What the system measures (pacing, pauses, filler words, navigation)
  - How feedback is generated (AI interpretation of metrics)
  - What constitutes good presentation skills
  
- **System Understanding of User Goals**: 
  - AI interprets metrics in context of presentation improvement
  - Understands that different metric combinations indicate different issues
  - Adapts feedback based on user's progress patterns

- **Convergence Over Time**:
  - As users practice multiple attempts, their understanding converges with system's measurements
  - Session-level analysis shows this convergence
  - Users learn what matters for good presentations

**How Our System Supports This**:
- Shows both raw metrics and AI interpretation (builds user mental model)
- Consistent feedback across attempts (helps convergence)
- Session-level analysis tracks patterns (demonstrates shared understanding)

---

#### 2. **LLMs as Zero-Shot Human Models (Zhang & Soh, 2023)**
**Citation**: Zhang, B., & Soh, H. (2023). Large language models as zero-shot human models for human-robot interaction. 2023 IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS).

**Core Idea**: Use LLMs to predict human behavior in collaborative tasks without task-specific training

**Relevance to Our System**: MEDIUM

**Connection**:
- While our system doesn't predict future actions, it uses LLMs for similar purposes:
  - **Commonsense reasoning about human behavior**: Understanding that "high filler words + fast pace = nervousness"
  - **Contextual interpretation**: LLM understands presentation context and human communication patterns
  - **Zero-shot capability**: No fine-tuning needed, uses general knowledge about presentations

**Key Difference**:
- Zhang & Soh: Predicts what human will do next
- Our System: Interprets what human did and why it matters

**Supporting Evidence**:
- LLMs capture commonsense structure of human tasks
- Can reason about temporal order and context
- Provide implicit understanding without explicit modeling

---

#### 3. **Cross-Training (Nikolaidis & Shah, 2013)**
**Citation**: Nikolaidis, S., & Shah, J. (2013). Human-robot cross-training: computational formulation, modeling and evaluation of a human team training strategy. Proceedings of the 8th ACM/IEEE International Conference on Human-Robot Interaction.

**Relevance to Our System**: LOW

**Why Not Applicable**:
- Cross-training involves team members learning each other's roles
- Our system is a feedback/coaching tool, not a collaborative task
- No role-swapping between user and AI
- User doesn't "train" the AI, AI provides feedback to user

## Lecture 04: AI-Mediated Communication (2025)

### Key Theories Identified

#### 1. **Hancock et al. (2020) - AI-Mediated Communication Framework**
**Citation**: Hancock, J. T., Naaman, M., & Levy, K. (2020). AI-Mediated Communication: Definition, Research Agenda, and Ethical Considerations. Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication, 25(1), 89-100.

**Definition**: "Interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication goals"

**Key Dimensions**:
- Magnitude: Extent of AI changes to messages
- Media type: Text, audio, video
- Optimization goal: What AI optimizes for
- Autonomy: Degree of AI independence
- Role orientation: Sender vs. receiver role

**Relevance to Our System**: HIGH

**Application**:
- Our system is AI-mediated communication (AI operates on behalf of user to enhance presentation)
- **Magnitude**: Moderate - AI interprets and provides feedback, doesn't generate content
- **Media type**: Multi-modal (audio + text + navigation)
- **Optimization goal**: Presentation quality and improvement
- **Autonomy**: Low - user controls when to analyze and view feedback
- **Role orientation**: Sender-focused (enhancing user's presentation)

---

#### 2. **Arnold et al. (2020) - Predictive Text Effects on Writing**
**Citation**: Arnold, K. C., Chauncey, K., & Gajos, K. Z. (2020). Predictive text encourages predictable writing. Proceedings of the 25th International Conference on Intelligent User Interfaces, 128-138.

**Key Findings**:
- Predictive suggestions encourage predictable word choices
- Writers conform to suggested words (substitution nudge)
- Suggestions can cause word skipping (skip nudge)
- Effects are subconscious - writers don't clearly perceive them
- Shorter, less thoughtful content when suggestions are visible

**Relevance to Our System**: MEDIUM

**Application**:
- **Cautionary Design Principle**: Our feedback should not overly constrain user behavior
- We provide feedback AFTER presentation, not during (avoiding real-time influence)
- Feedback is interpretive, not prescriptive (doesn't suggest exact words)
- User maintains agency and ownership of their presentation style

**Design Implications**:
- Avoid real-time suggestions that might constrain natural expression
- Provide feedback that supports reflection, not immediate conformity
- Maintain user's authentic voice and style

---

#### 3. **Jakesch et al. (2023) - Co-Writing with Opinionated LLMs**
**Citation**: Jakesch, M., Bhat, A., Buschek, D., Zalmanson, L., & Naaman, M. (2023). Co-writing with opinionated language models affects users' views. Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, 1-15.

**Key Findings**:
- Opinionated language models can influence users' written opinions
- Users' post-interaction opinions shift toward model's opinion
- Users often unaware of model's influence
- Suggestion acceptance correlates with opinion change
- Users assess opinionated models as more knowledgeable

**Relevance to Our System**: MEDIUM-HIGH

**Application**:
- **Ethical Consideration**: Our feedback should be objective and balanced
- Avoid opinionated or biased feedback that could influence user's presentation style inappropriately
- Ensure feedback is based on objective metrics, not subjective preferences
- Transparency about what the system measures and why

**Design Implications**:
- Base feedback on objective metrics (pacing, pauses, filler words)
- Avoid subjective style preferences
- Maintain transparency about feedback sources
- Support user's authentic presentation style

---

#### 4. **Jakesch et al. (2023) - Human Heuristics for AI-Generated Language**
**Citation**: Jakesch, M., Hancock, J. T., & Naaman, M. (2023). Human heuristics for AI-generated language are flawed. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 120(11), e2208839120.

**Key Finding**: People struggle to distinguish AI-generated text from human text, using flawed heuristics

**Relevance to Our System**: LOW-MEDIUM

**Application**:
- Users may not always recognize when feedback is AI-generated vs. human-like
- Transparency about AI involvement is important
- Users should understand the source of feedback

---

#### 5. **Dash et al. (2025) - AI-Paraphrased Information Persuasion**
**Citation**: Dash, S., Xu, Y., Jalbert, M., & Spiro, E. S. (2025). The persuasive potential of AI-paraphrased information at scale. PNAS nexus, 4(7), pgaf207.

**Key Finding**: AI-paraphrased messages can be more persuasive than identical repeated messages (AI-Pasta vs. Copy-Pasta)

**Relevance to Our System**: LOW

**Application**:
- Not directly applicable (our system doesn't paraphrase or manipulate messages)
- General ethical consideration: avoid manipulation through AI-generated content

---

### Summary for Lecture 04

## Lecture 06: Retrieving and Presenting Search Results (2025)

### Key Theories Identified

#### 1. **Shahaf, Guestrin, & Horvitz (2012) - Metro Maps of Information**
**Citation**: Shahaf, D., Guestrin, C., & Horvitz, E. (2012). Trains of thought: Generating information maps. Proceedings of the 21st international conference on World Wide Web, 899-908.

**Core Concept**: A method for presenting complex information through structured "metro maps" - sets of coherent storylines (paths) that can be connected through shared documents. Each line follows a coherent narrative thread.

**Key Criteria**:
- **Coherence**: Chains are as strong as their weakest component; based on shared important words between documents
- **Coverage**: Map should cover important aspects and encourage diversity
- **Connectivity**: Number of intersections between storylines

**Relevance to Our System**: MEDIUM

**Application**:
- **Organizing Feedback**: Our system could organize feedback into coherent narratives rather than just listing metrics
- **Multiple Storylines**: Different feedback aspects (pacing, clarity, engagement) could form different "lines"
- **Coherence**: Feedback points should connect logically (e.g., "fast pace + high filler words = nervousness")
- **Coverage**: Ensure feedback covers all important aspects of presentation
- **Connectivity**: Show relationships between different feedback dimensions

**Design Implications**:
- Present feedback as coherent narratives, not just isolated metrics
- Show connections between different aspects of performance
- Organize feedback into meaningful storylines (e.g., "nervousness indicators", "engagement patterns")
- Help users understand the "story" of their presentation performance

**Potential Implementation**:
- Session-level analysis could present trends as coherent narratives
- Connect related feedback points (e.g., "Your pacing improved, which likely reduced filler words")
- Show how different metrics relate to each other in meaningful ways

## Lecture 07: Interpretable Machine Learning (2025)

### Key Theories Identified

#### 1. **Lipton (2016) - The Mythos of Model Interpretability**
**Citation**: Lipton, Z. C. (2016). The mythos of model interpretability. arXiv preprint arXiv:1606.03490.

**Core Concept**: Interpretability is inconsistently defined across papers. Key reasons for needing interpretability include: **Trust**, **Causality**, **Transferability**, and **Informativeness**.

**Key Dimensions**:
- **Transparency**: Understanding how the model works (simulatability, decomposability, algorithmic transparency)
- **Explainability**: Model's ability to offer post-hoc explanations

**Relevance to Our System**: HIGH

**Application**:
- **Trust**: Users need to trust the AI feedback - does it know when it's uncertain? Are we comfortable with the model?
- **Informativeness**: The real purpose is to aid users in making decisions about their presentation, so explanations carry extra value
- **Transparency**: Users should understand what the system measures and how it interprets metrics
- **Explainability**: System provides post-hoc explanations of why certain feedback is given

**Design Implications**:
- Show both raw metrics and AI interpretation (transparency)
- Explain why issues matter (e.g., "high filler words + fast pace = nervousness")
- Acknowledge uncertainty when appropriate
- Make the reasoning process understandable

---

#### 2. **Ribeiro et al. (2017) - LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations)**
**Citation**: Ribeiro, M. T., Singh, S., & Guestrin, C. (2016). "Why should I trust you?" Explaining the predictions of any classifier. Proceedings of the 22nd ACM SIGKDD international conference on knowledge discovery and data mining, 1135-1144.

**Core Concept**: Create a simpler local model around the instance that needs to be explained. Balance between model complexity and faithfulness to the original model.

**Relevance to Our System**: MEDIUM

**Application**:
- **Local Explanations**: Our system could provide local explanations for specific feedback points
- **Feature Importance**: Show which metrics (features) are most important for a given feedback interpretation
- **Simplified Reasoning**: Break down complex AI interpretations into simpler, understandable components

**Design Implications**:
- For each feedback point, show which metrics contributed most
- Explain why specific combinations of metrics lead to certain interpretations
- Provide simplified reasoning that users can understand

---

#### 3. **Kim et al. (2017) - TCAV (Testing with Concept Activation Vectors)**
**Citation**: Kim, B., Wattenberg, M., Gilmer, J., Cai, C., Wexler, J., Viegas, F., & Sayres, R. (2018). Interpretability beyond feature attribution: Quantitative testing with concept activation vectors (TCAV). International conference on machine learning, 2668-2677.

**Core Concept**: Test what concepts (high-level ideas) a model uses for predictions, rather than just which features. Uses concept activation vectors to quantify how important a concept is for a prediction.

**Relevance to Our System**: MEDIUM

**Application**:
- **Concept-Based Explanations**: Our system could explain feedback in terms of high-level concepts (e.g., "nervousness", "engagement", "clarity") rather than just raw metrics
- **Domain Alignment**: Ensure AI interpretations align with human understanding of presentation concepts
- **Validation**: Verify that the model uses concepts consistent with expert knowledge

**Design Implications**:
- Frame feedback in terms of presentation concepts users understand
- Show how metrics map to concepts (e.g., "fast pace + high filler words → nervousness concept")
- Validate that AI interpretations align with presentation coaching expertise

---

#### 4. **Miller (2017) - Explanation in AI: Insights from Social Sciences**
**Citation**: Miller, T. (2017). Explanation in artificial intelligence: Insights from the social sciences. Artificial Intelligence, 267, 1-38.

**Key Insights from Social Sciences**:
- **Contrastive Explanations**: People ask "why P and not Q?" - explanations should be contrastive
- **Attribution Theory**: People attribute behaviors based on beliefs, desires, intentions
- **Explanation Selection**: People select 1-2 causes, not complete causal chains
- **Explanation Evaluation**: People prefer simpler, more general, coherent explanations
- **Explanation as Conversation**: Explanations follow Grice Maxims (quality, quantity, manner)

**Relevance to Our System**: HIGH

**Application**:
- **Contrastive Explanations**: "Why is this attempt better than the previous one?" rather than just "This is good"
- **Simple Explanations**: Provide 1-2 key reasons, not exhaustive lists
- **Conversational**: Explanations should follow conversational principles
- **Coherent**: Explanations should be consistent with user's prior knowledge about presentations

**Design Implications**:
- Frame feedback as contrastive ("Your pacing improved compared to attempt 1")
- Keep explanations simple and focused (1-2 key points)
- Make explanations conversational and natural
- Ensure explanations align with common presentation knowledge

---

#### 5. **Grice (1975) - Grice Maxims for Explanations**
**Citation**: Grice, H. P. (1975). Logic and conversation. In P. Cole & J. L. Morgan (Eds.), Syntax and semantics: Vol. 3. Speech acts (pp. 41-58). Academic Press.

**Core Principles**:
- **Quality**: Only say what you believe (truthful)
- **Quantity**: Only say as much as necessary
- **Manner**: Say it in a clear, nice way

**Relevance to Our System**: MEDIUM-HIGH

**Application**:
- **Quality**: Feedback should be truthful and based on actual metrics
- **Quantity**: Don't overwhelm users with too much information
- **Manner**: Present feedback clearly and constructively

**Design Implications**:
- Ensure feedback is accurate and based on real measurements
- Prioritize most important feedback points
- Present feedback in a clear, constructive manner

---

## Lecture 09: Fairness in Machine Learning (2025)

**Citation**: Green, B., & Chen, Y. (2019). The principles and limits of algorithm-in-the-loop decision making. Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 3(CSCW), 1-24.

#### 2. **Kim et al. (2024) - Uncertainty Expression in LLMs**
**Citation**: Kim, S. S. Y., et al. (2024). "I'm Not Sure, But...": Examining the Impact of Large Language Models' Uncertainty Expression on User Reliance and Trust. Proceedings of the 2024 ACM conference on fairness, accountability, and transparency.

**Core Concept**: Studies how different uncertainty expressions in LLM responses affect user reliance, trust, and task performance. Examines whether uncertainty expression helps users calibrate reliance rather than blindly defer.

**Key Findings**:
- **No uncertainty expression**: Highest user reliance, increased risk of over-reliance
- **Implicit uncertainty (hedging)**: Reduced trust without reliably improving decision quality, often interpreted as lack of competence
- **Explicit uncertainty + rationale**: Best calibration of reliance, improved decision accuracy, maintained trust while discouraging blind deference

**Relevance to Our System**: HIGH

**Application**:
- **LLM-Mediated Feedback**: Our system uses LLMs to generate feedback, so uncertainty expression is directly relevant
- **User Reliance**: Users need to know when to trust AI feedback and when to question it
- **Calibration**: Explicit uncertainty + rationale helps users make better decisions about their presentations
- **Trust**: Maintaining trust while avoiding over-reliance is crucial

**Design Implications**:
- Express uncertainty explicitly when AI is uncertain about feedback
- Provide rationale for uncertainty (e.g., "I'm not entirely certain, but based on the metrics...")
- Avoid implicit hedging that might be interpreted as incompetence
- Help users calibrate their reliance on AI feedback

**Citation**: Lucy, L., et al. (2024). "One-Size-Fits-All"? Examining Expectations around What Constitute "Fair" or "Good" NLG System Behaviors. Proceedings of the 2024 Conference of the North American Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics: Human Language Technologies (Volume 1: Long Papers).

**Core Concept**: Examines user expectations of what constitutes "fair" or "good" behavior from Natural Language Generation (NLG) systems. Studies whether expectations are consistent across users and contexts, and whether they align with technical fairness definitions.

**Key Findings**:
